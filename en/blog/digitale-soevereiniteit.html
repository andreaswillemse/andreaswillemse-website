Sovereignty November 2026 13 min read

Who can pull your plug?

With every system you use, someone can pull the plug. Digital sovereignty means you decide who that is. Below: what it is, why it matters, why it is so hard, and what you can do tomorrow.

Let's go back to 1875. In Paris, seventeen countries sit around a table and agree on one thing: how long a metre is. The kind of meeting you would rather skip. Yet you still use the outcome every day. Once, every town had its own ell, its own pound, its own measure for grain and its own opinion about the length of a foot. If you wanted to sell something to the next town, you started with an argument about the measure. And whoever set the measure usually won that argument. Paris put an end to that with one agreement: the measure belongs to everyone, and to no one in particular. Since then there has been nothing left to argue about, and so nothing left to win. Today we call that an open standard (in Dutch).

That is exactly what Europe needs to do with its IT infrastructure. As long as every provider uses its own measure, you are stuck with your supplier, and the power lies with the biggest. If all EU countries agree on the same open standards, you can switch whenever you want. That costs the hyperscalers part of their power. Behind all of this is the same question as in 1875: who sets the rules you live under?

That touches on sovereignty. An open standard makes sure you can leave. Sovereignty is about who is in charge while you stay. A sovereign country makes its own laws and lets no one else dictate them. Digital sovereignty is the same idea, but for organisations and their systems and data: are you in charge of them, or is someone else?

In my previous piece I wrote that there's always someone at the controls. People are responsible for what systems do, always. This piece is about the question that follows straight from that. Who is that person? Which country are they in? And do they listen to you?

What it is

Sovereignty has layers. In my previous piece I described six: where the data is stored, which law applies, who manages the system, where the model comes from (if there is AI in it), whether the software is open source (then you can develop it further yourself), and where the hardware comes from. The most important lesson: location is not enough. Data in a Dutch data centre owned by an American company falls under both Dutch and American law. The American government can demand it from the parent company.

For an organisation, three more questions come on top of that.

  1. Who operates the system? Who has the admin rights, who holds the keys, who can pull the plug?
  2. Is there a way out? Is your data in open file formats, does the export work, and have you ever rehearsed a move? Without a way out, every other measure is decoration.
  3. Does your software talk to other software? Open standards decide whether you can replace one part without breaking the rest.

State Secretary (a junior minister) Willemijn Aerdts summed it up in July (in Dutch): sovereignty means being able to make choices. Choosing another supplier when you need to. Choosing for yourself who can get to your data. I put it this way: sovereignty is being able to walk away without everything grinding to a halt. Everything below is a test of that one question.

Why it matters

You don't have to leave The Hague for examples. Three times, the International Criminal Court (ICC) shows what happens when someone else is at the controls.

One account. In February 2025 the Trump administration imposed sanctions on chief prosecutor Karim Khan, after the arrest warrants against Netanyahu and Gallant. A few months later, Khan saw this when he logged in: "Your account is currently blocked." According to the Dutch newspaper de Volkskrant (in Dutch), that was a decision by Microsoft, which was carrying out the sanctions. Microsoft says the shutdown was the result of sanctions (in Dutch), and not a decision to stop serving the court. Who exactly pressed the button has never been cleared up. According to Dutch reports, the court was given a choice: cut Khan off, or lose the email of the whole organisation. Microsoft does not confirm that. What is certain: a company in Redmond carried out a decision from Washington, and a court in The Hague had no say in it.

One person. On 20 August 2025 the French judge Nicolas Guillou heard that he was on the American sanctions list. His signature was on the arrest warrant. After that, as he told de Volkskrant (in Dutch): Expedia blocked his booking, his French bank cancelled his credit card, his French health insurer Axa quietly stopped paying out, and UPS refused a parcel. "Those sanctions are a time machine." His warning: "Someone can decide with one signature that you are completely cut off from all those tools. And I don't just mean platforms, but also all your memory: the cloud, your mailbox."

One organisation. On 9 October 2026 the US put the court itself on the sanctions list. Secretary of State Rubio wants to tear it down "brick by brick". From April 2027, American companies may no longer do business with the court. The same day, the US Treasury quietly issued an exemption for business software, as de Volkskrant found out (in Dutch). Microsoft, Google and Amazon may keep supplying, until Washington decides otherwise. Registrar Osvaldo Zavala Giler: "You cannot base the continuity of your operations on a favour that is granted and can be withdrawn at any moment." The court is replacing Microsoft (in Dutch) with OpenDesk, an open source workplace from the German government.

It's not just Trump. In April 2022 Amsterdam Trade Bank went bankrupt. The bank was healthy, but it had a Russian parent and ended up on the American and British sanctions lists after the invasion of Ukraine. Microsoft cut off the email, Amazon terminated the storage, a supplier revoked access to the payment system. Within two weeks it was over. The trustees had to go to court to force Microsoft to release the bank's records. I supported those sanctions, and so did most Dutch people. But the mechanism is the same. Today you agree with the sanction. Tomorrow you are the target.

Even people with nothing to fear get scared. On 26 August 2026 the US put the Italian hosting collective Autistici/Inventati on its terror list, a volunteer group that hosts websites and email for some twenty thousand left-wing groups. The Dutch newspaper NRC described (in Dutch) what happened next. PayPal gone. The .org domain struck off, because the registry is in the US. A few days later the Italian bank blocked the account, afraid of being thrown out of the Visa network. Nobody in Brussels said a word about it. The Dutch hosting company ProcoliX then pulled out of extra work for the ICC: "We are too vulnerable." A director who wants to stay anonymous: "Everyone is afraid of being next. And yes, that is exactly the effect they want." That is the difference between an outage and a sanction. An outage hits you. A sanction also hits everyone who helps you.

The same goes for AI. On 12 June 2026 Anthropic shut off access to its two newest models to comply with export rules from the US Department of Commerce. On 1 July the door opened again. Three weeks without a model, because of a decision in Washington. I use their models myself, including for writing. That is exactly why this example is here.

And for your data. The American CLOUD Act requires American companies to hand over data when the American government asks for it with a valid warrant, wherever that data is stored. It is one of the reasons for the new European cloud law.

The Netherlands is waking up. In April 2026 the Dutch central government signed a framework agreement with StackIT (in Dutch), the cloud of the German owner of Lidl, with a way out in the contract in case the supplier ends up in non-European hands. In May the Dutch government blocked (in Dutch) the American company Kyndryl from taking over Solvinity, the company behind the data of DigiD, the Dutch digital identity for public services. On 3 July it tightened the Dutch government's cloud policy (in Dutch): email and document management do not belong in the public cloud, and source data from the government's base registries may not go there at all. And the Dutch central government is building its own cloud (in Dutch) in its own data centres, on open source. It is a start, nothing more. Most governments and companies still run on American systems, and that own cloud still has to be built.

The midterms don't change that. On 3 November the Democrats won both the House and the Senate (in Dutch). Good news for the rule of law. But Trump remains president until January 2029 and keeps his veto. And the sanctions against the ICC come from the White House and the Treasury, without any role for Congress. Wouter Welling (in Dutch) does the maths: risk is probability times impact. The probability of a new blow may have become a little smaller. The impact of our dependence is exactly as big.

Why it is so hard

If it is so necessary, why isn't everyone doing it? Because practice is stubborn.

Availability is expensive. A system that must never go down needs a second data centre, duplicate hardware and people who wake up at night. The big providers already have that. You have to build it. Although even with them a large part of the internet sometimes goes down, as with Amazon in October 2025.

Contracts run long. Organisations are tied to licences and framework agreements for years. Marietje Schaake points out that almost all contracts contain clauses for exceptional situations. The question is whether you dare to use them.

Nothing can go down during the move either. The first point was about an ordinary working day. This one is about the switch itself. A hospital, a municipality or an online shop doesn't just close for a weekend to move. You renovate while the shop is open.

Moving takes people and time. An average IT migration takes six months to three years, says Ludo Baauw of the cloud provider Intermax. Once you are in, you don't just get out again. (in Dutch)

Running it yourself sounds old-fashioned. We have only just all moved to the cloud. Going back to your own servers feels like a step backwards. That feeling is half the resistance.

And there is more.

European providers are smaller. They now have around 15 per cent of their own cloud market, down from 29 per cent in 2017. Amazon, Microsoft and Google together have 70 per cent and invest around ten billion euros a quarter in Europe. Every euro that goes there builds capacity and knowledge on the other side of the ocean. If you switch to a European provider, you get a smaller catalogue and have to do more yourself.

And nobody buys European. Bert Hubert (in Dutch) estimates that less than 1 per cent of our email is on European systems. Public tenders ask for Microsoft licences, so for thirty years European industry has barely had a signal to buy and is, according to him, 'all but dead'. Digital autonomy is in the coalition agreement. The national budget (the Miljoenennota) set aside zero euros for it. His summary: everyone wants change, but nobody wants to change.

The people are scarce. The government works with Microsoft, hires people for their knowledge of Microsoft, and they in turn advise Microsoft. One insider told de Volkskrant: "People do feel the political pressure, but in practice they only know Microsoft." People who can build and run a platform themselves are rare and expensive. The knowledge has leaked away. AI might shrink that shortage: a small team with good tools builds and manages more than it used to. And the shop floor wants to keep its familiar buttons.

The big providers are already certified. ISO 27001, NEN 7510, you name it: Amazon, Microsoft and Google already have them all. A platform of your own still has to earn them. For an auditor, the American is the safe choice.

The word 'sovereign' is being misused. Every big provider now sells something 'sovereign', sometimes no more than sovereign lite. Amazon has a separate European 'sovereign cloud', Microsoft its EU Data Boundary and a copy of its source code in a Swiss vault. Useful: the data stays in Europe and European staff manage it. But the parent company falls under American law, and the law travels with it. Baauw on that vault: "What does a step like that do for me as a customer? Nothing. It doesn't change the geopolitical situation."

Attention is fading. This is the biggest risk of the coming months. After the midterms you already hear it: the danger has passed, sovereignty is a vague concept, we have other things on our minds. That is exactly when organisations sign up again for five years with the familiar supplier, because it is familiar, cheap and quickly arranged. And the next time Washington reaches for the controls, it all starts over again. Welling (in Dutch) warned about this in early November.

What you can do tomorrow

Four questions I now ask about every app, every project and every organisation:

  1. Who can cut us off, and which law does that person fall under?
  2. What happens on Monday morning if this supplier drops out?
  3. How sensitive is this application, and what level of sovereignty goes with it?
  4. Can we leave? Is the data in open formats, and have we ever rehearsed the move?

And four steps:

  1. Map your dependencies, layer by layer. Login, email, documents, data, AI, and your payments too. You will be shocked at how much hangs on one account.
  2. Classify your applications by sensitivity. The European Commission has a scale for that, the SEAL levels from my previous piece. The Cloud and AI Development Act, which Brussels proposed on 3 June 2026, wants to make such a test mandatory for governments and critical companies. According to the Commission, around 70 per cent of government contracts fall into the lowest level, and 1 per cent into the highest. Sovereignty is a scale, not a switch.
  3. Arrange a way out for your most sensitive applications, and rehearse it. A migration plan that has never been tested is a wish.
  4. Buy European where you can, even if the product has fewer buttons. Without buyers there will be no European supply. That is Hubert's call.

Judge Guillou had one more lesson (in Dutch). European solutions exist, but they don't talk to each other. Each with its own measure, in other words. Which brings us back to that meeting in Paris. The recipe from 1875 still works: one measure, belonging to everyone and to no one in particular. For software, that means open standards for email, documents, identity and data, agreed by all EU countries. If all European systems use them, any organisation can switch supplier without rebuilding everything. That breaks part of the power of the hyperscalers. Someone can still pull your plug. But that plug now fits another socket too.

Full sovereignty hardly exists. You always depend on someone. The difference lies in knowing on whom, and whether you chose that yourself. Sovereignty means you decide who that is.
